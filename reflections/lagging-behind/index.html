Lagging behind?

Two and a half years after graduation, still no job — and the feeling that it was easy for everyone else. Five old verses have a lot to say about this exact moment.

01 — Waiting Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.1.9

Waiting is not wasted.

A boy waits three nights at Death's door — and every night becomes a gift.

Night 0 of 3

Nachiketā's father, performing a great sacrifice, was giving away cows too old to give milk. The boy asked, three times, “To whom will you give me?” In anger the father said: “To Death I give you.”

So Nachiketā went to the house of Yama, the lord of Death. Yama was away. The boy waited at the door for three nights, with nothing to eat. When Yama returned, he did not tell the boy he was late. He said this:

What it says to you · my reading

The nights Nachiketā spent waiting were not deducted from his life. They were counted — one boon for each. And the boy who arrived “late” went on to receive the deepest teaching in the Upanishads, one nobody else in the story was given.

Your two and a half years can be counted the same way, if you look at them honestly: which interviews went wrong, what you now understand about the work you want, what you can do now that you couldn't then. Waiting you learn from is not wasted.

02 — Comparison Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 3.1.1–2

Two birds. One tree.

One keeps tasting the fruit and grieves. The other only watches — and is free.

This image is so old it comes from the Ṛgveda, and the Upanishads keep returning to it.

What it says to you · my reading

The grieving bird is the one tasting every fruit: their offer letters, their salaries, their “excited to share” posts. Each taste turns into a judgement about yourself. The verse names the feeling exactly — anīśayā śocati, it grieves from a sense of powerlessness.

Its sorrow ends not when it wins the fruit, but when it sees the other bird: the part of you that watches calmly and is not made of results. “It was easy for them” is something you see from outside. You see their fruit, never their struggle.

answers “for them it was easy”
03 — Effort Īśā Upaniṣad 2

Keep doing your work.

“There is no other way than this.” Not a race — a hundred-year horizon.

The Īśā is only eighteen verses long. Its second verse is about how to live in the world while working in it.

What it says to you · my reading

The verse does not say “act if you will win”, or “act if you are on schedule”. It says keep doing your work, and that work done this way does not bind you. The weight comes from tying your worth to the result — not from the effort.

And it sets the horizon at a hundred years. Look at the grid below: the gap that feels enormous is two and a half squares.

04 — The choice Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.2.1–2

Choose the good, not the easy.

Śreyas, the good, and preyas, the pleasant, both come to every person. The wise one tells them apart.

Two roads. Which one?

Yama offers Nachiketā every pleasure — wealth, long life, music — in place of his question. The boy refuses. Yama is pleased, and says:

The verse just before it · Kaṭha 1.2.1

अन्यच्छ्रेयोऽन्यदुतैव प्रेयस्ते उभे नानार्थे पुरुषँ सिनीतः ।
तयोः श्रेय आददानस्य साधु
भवति हीयतेऽर्थाद्य उ प्रेयो वृणीते ॥

anyacchreyo’nyadutaiva preyaste ubhe nānārthe puruṣam̐ sinītaḥ ।
tayoḥ śreya ādadānasya sādhu
bhavati hīyate’rthādya u preyo vṛṇīte ॥

“Different is (that which is) preferable; and different, indeed, is the pleasurable. These two, serving different purposes, blind man. Good accrues to him who, of these two, chooses the preferable. He who chooses the pleasurable falls from the goal.”

What it says to you · my reading

Right now the pleasant choice is to decide it's pointless and stop. It feels like relief, and the verse says plainly what it costs: “he who chooses the pleasurable falls from the goal.”

The good choice is harder and quieter: begin again, without needing it to feel good yet. Notice the key word, vivinakti — the wise one examines both and separates them. You don't have to feel brave. You only have to see which road is which.

05 — Begin Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.3.14

Arise. Awake. Find those who know.

The most famous line in the Kaṭha — and it admits the path is hard.

uttiṣṭhata · jāgrata

What it says to you · my reading

Three verbs, in order: arise (get up from where you've sunk), awake (see your situation clearly, not through the fog of comparison), learn by approaching — prāpya varān, go to those who are further along.

And then, honestly: the path is “sharp as a razor's edge.” If it feels difficult, that doesn't mean you're failing. It's the path as described. Don't walk it alone — find people who can guide you: seniors, alumni, people already doing the work you want.

यो वै भूमा तत् सुखम् । नाल्पे सुखम् अस्ति ।

yo vai bhūmā tat sukham · nālpe sukham asti

“That which is infinite, is alone happiness. There is no happiness in anything finite.”
Chāndogya Upaniṣad 7.23.1 · tr. Swami Swahananda

A job matters a great deal. But your worth was never meant to rest on one offer letter.
